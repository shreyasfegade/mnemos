AI agents write their own long-term memory to an encrypted store on the user's device, then retrieve
it by semantic search. We study an adversary that obtains the storage layer partway through such a
store's life --- through a synchronized backup or repeated disk imaging --- observes later activity,
and can steer the agent's inputs, but holds only stored state for records written earlier. We show
that historical confidentiality, the safety of data written before the compromise, is a distinct
system-level property that encryption and existing snapshot or history-independence notions do not
provide. For encrypted vector stores that place records by content, labels the adversary induces
through new writes transfer to the earlier records, exposing both equally; a transfer lemma names its
precondition, that the layout disclose which partition holds an earlier record, which we confirm on a
real deployed store, pgvector under an encrypted filesystem. A store that seals that channel is
reached by three others: retained file versions, capacity refusals, and derived writes, where an
agent that deduplicates turns the mere occurrence of a write into a membership oracle for earlier
facts. We formalize dual-epoch security as an adaptive, leakage-parameterized game, prove sufficient
conditions covering capacity overflow, separate it from breach resistance and history independence,
and add a reactive variant whose composition theorem covers the self-writing agent. A memory-resident
store with content-oblivious placement and a clocked, write-only-oblivious flush closes every channel
against a write observer; its flush costs kilobytes rather than megabytes, and its writes reveal only
a clock. A partitioned sealed store covers larger corpora with its residual channels stated. Every
reported number is reproduced by a released artifact.
