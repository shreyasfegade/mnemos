\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:eval}

The evaluation asks how far structure transfer reaches the earlier cohort in realistic settings
(\cref{sec:eval-transfer,sec:eval-real,sec:eval-induction}), whether the conditions of
\cref{thm:main} hold in the compiled engines and which are load-bearing
(\cref{sec:eval-game,sec:eval-audit}), which channels still reach cohort H
(\cref{sec:eval-cohorth,sec:eval-rw}), and what the designs cost
(\cref{sec:eval-perf,sec:eval-recall}).

\paragraph{Setup}
Records are embedded with \texttt{bge-base-en-v1.5} \cite{xiao2024cpack}. Corpora are D2, eight
BEIR domains \cite{thakur2021beir} used as a topic taxonomy; D3, a synthetic procedural-memory
corpus; 13{,}894 timestamped \texttt{kubernetes} issues labeled by their \texttt{sig/*} area; 20
Newsgroups; and memories a local model wrote from those issues. Advantage is accuracy minus chance,
untruncated, for an adversary that guesses wherever it has no label, since one that abstains scores
below chance merely for declining to guess. Intervals are 95\%, over each experiment's independent
units: bootstrap from twenty units, Student-$t$ below. Attacks read only the engine's emitted event
stream, which is the storage adversary's view, and every number in the text is generated from the
stored results. Outcomes registered before a run are marked as such, including two that failed
(\cref{sec:eval-induction}, and query equality at $s = 4$ in the supplementary material).

\subsection{Structure Transfer, and What Drift Is Worth}
\label{sec:eval-transfer}
\label{sec:eval-drift}

A naive form of this attack groups induced records by the partition they landed in before
intersecting, an attribution the adversary does not have, and scores the post-compromise cohort on
the induced records themselves. Neither is done here: the estimators are frequency analysis and an
expectation-maximization estimator that never see the true partition of an induced record, the
induced records are excluded when scoring, and the oracle is retained only as an upper bound.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_drift.pdf}
\caption{What subject drift is actually worth. The horizontal axis is the time gap between cohort H
and the content the adversary induces with; bands are 95\% bootstrap intervals over $k$-means
seeds. The protection drift appears to offer is a property of the adversary's choice of induction
material, not of the data: an adversary that supplies archival content from cohort H's own era
recovers the advantage in full.}
\label{fig:drift}
\end{figure}


On the timestamped \texttt{kubernetes} corpus, splitting chronologically rather than i.i.d.\ lowers
the advantage on the earlier cohort substantially, reproducing the effect drift is supposed to
provide. \Cref{fig:drift} then asks what that split leaves open: the adversary chooses what to induce,
and for a public tracker it can induce with archival content. The curve is the
advantage against the \emph{time gap} between cohort H and the induced content. The honest reading
is the one the gap-zero row supports: drift protects early records against an adversary that
induces with current material, and considerably less against one that does not. The coarse-taxonomy
arm is scored against coarse labels, so its chance rate is one in four rather than one in twelve.

\subsection{Deployed Stores, and Time-Placed Controls}
\label{sec:eval-real}

The attack was run against four deployed stores (Chroma, FAISS, LanceDB and sqlite-vec), an indexed
LanceDB variant and an append-only control, each inside a \texttt{gocryptfs} volume, whose
ciphertext mirrors the plaintext file structure block for block. Two choices matter. Block labels
are fitted on one half of the induced writes and scored on the other, because fitting and scoring
on the same writes yields near-perfect localisation by construction. And induced writes are
interleaved across topics: several of these stores append at a tail, so consecutive writes share
blocks whatever their content, and issuing a topic's writes together makes write order masquerade
as content placement.

With both in place the result is negative, and it is worth stating plainly: the attack does not
reach the pre-compromise cohort in any of the six configurations at this budget. Held-out
localisation sits at chance throughout, and no interval on \advhist{} reaches above
$\REsixWorstHi$ over \REsixSeeds{} seeds. Two mechanisms produce that. In the time-placed stores,
and in those that rewrite their whole index on every save, an induced write touches none of the
blocks that hold cohort-H records, so the label map covers none of the earlier cohort. Chroma is
the instructive case: its HNSW insert updates existing nodes' neighbor lists, so induced writes do
touch cohort-H blocks, and the labels still carry no topic information, \advhist{} $=
\REsixChroma$; which blocks an insert touches is a function of graph structure and insertion order,
not only of content. The supplementary material tabulates every store.

What the deployed stores establish is therefore the premise of \cref{lem:transfer} rather than its
conclusion: transfer needs a layout that discloses which partition holds a given stored record, and
none of these stores discloses that at the granularity a block-level observer sees. The
growing-region layout of \cref{sec:transfer} does, which is why the attack works against it. A
larger budget, a finer block size, or a store that exposes per-list offsets could change this; the
measurement bounds what was observed, not what is possible.

\subsection{Real-Agent Induction and the Deduplication Oracle}
\label{sec:eval-induction}
\label{sec:eval-dedup}

\input{tables/e2_agent}

The attack assumes an adversary who supplies content causes the agent to write about it.
\Cref{tab:e2} runs the whole attack against memories a local model actually authored from
adversary-supplied issues, rather than against corpus passages standing in for them. It also sweeps
the burst size: a writing turn yields several memories on one subject within milliseconds, so a
realistic flush carries a small same-topic burst rather than a single record.

The growing layout is the positive control. It discloses each record's partition, and labels
learned from these memories transfer to the earlier cohort at every budget, so agent-written text
preserves the topic structure the attack relies on; without that row a null against the sealed
design could not be told apart from a harness that finds no structure at all. Against Mnemos-P
the harness supplies cohort-H membership, as a version-diff adversary would obtain it, so that row
isolates what padding does to labelling. It keeps a single-record attack near chance, no interval
reaching above $+\REtwoSingleMaxHi$, and the burst sweep shows why that is not the whole story.
Padding hides a flush's real regions among a fixed amount of cover, and a burst of same-topic
records multiplies the signal against that cover: at four records per turn the mean advantage is
\REtwoBurstRatio{} times the single-record value, peaking at $+\REtwoBurstMax$. We had expected
the padded design to stay within 0.05 of chance throughout, and registered that expectation before
running the burst arms; it does not. An agent that writes in bursts leaves the partitioned
design's write channel considerably more open than single-record measurements suggest.

The deduplication oracle of \cref{sec:attack-derived} is measured next, with every arm tabulated
in the supplementary material. The measurement has to
avoid a circularity the setting invites: prompting the agent to write about a retrieved subject and
then reporting that the follow-up write landed in the retrieved memory's partition holds by
construction and measures nothing. Here the adversary supplies a document and observes only
\emph{whether a write happened}. Against a store that flushes when records are staged, that is
the answer; against one that flushes on a clock, a flush happens either way. With a similarity
threshold deciding, the oracle is perfect. With a model deciding, as in mem0's pipeline, it is
noisy but open: \texttt{\REthreeModel} writes \REthreeLlmWriteMember{} of facts the store already
holds against \REthreeLlmWriteNonmember{} of new ones, an AUC of \REthreePLlmAUC{} against
Mnemos-P, and the clocked flush takes the same arm to \REthreeMLlmAUC. The separation is exactly
the one \cref{prop:derived} predicts, and it is why the proposition is stated about stores whose
operations depend on content or occurrence, rather than about storage designs in general.

\subsection{The Dual-Epoch Game}
\label{sec:eval-game}

\input{tables/e5_game}

\Cref{tab:e5} plays the game of \cref{def:dualepoch} against the compiled engines, with
admissibility \emph{verified} (every admissible arm asserts a zero refusal counter), held-out
evaluation, and a hand-built statistic beside the learned classifier in every arm; the
supplementary material explains why each choice is load-bearing.

The keyed permutation is the arm that settles a question the construction invites. Removing it,
which \cref{thm:main} does not require, leaves the game \emph{at chance} once histories are
genuinely admissible: what makes an identity permutation appear distinguishable is the refusal
channel showing through inadmissible histories, not the absence of the permutation. Removing S1 or
S2 remains perfectly distinguishable. The inadmissible arms show the refusal channel directly, and
show that it closes when a refused write performs the rewrites an accepted one would have.

Three arms test \cref{lem:slot}, and their outcomes were registered before they ran. Writing only
the placed slot, with the slot drawn uniformly, is at chance, \REfiveSlotRandom; so is keeping
whole-region rewrites but placing at the lowest free slot, \REfiveRegionFirstFree. Removing both,
so that the index of the one slot written is the region's current load, gives \REfiveSlotFirstFree.
Either protection alone suffices, as the lemma says, which makes S3 a condition on the pair
(placement rule, write granularity) rather than on the write granularity alone.

\subsection{The Reactive Game}
\label{sec:eval-reactive}

\Cref{def:dualepoch} lets the game distinguish a design from its ablations only through the leakage
the adversary chooses to provoke; a reader noted that it cannot, on its own, tell Mnemos-M from a
version of itself that places by content, because both are at chance when the adversary supplies
every operation. The reactive game of \cref{def:reactive} can, and \cref{thm:reactive} says it
should: it hands the post-$\tau$ operations to a feedback agent whose writes depend on what it
retrieves. We run it with a deduplicating agent --- it writes a supplied fact only if a near-duplicate
is not already stored --- and report the held-out AUC of the best trace statistic per design,
oriented on a training half of the probes and scored on the other. The prediction, registered before
the run, was that only the clock-only arms sit at chance.

The outcome is stark. The shipped memory-resident store is at chance, \REtwentyMSweep; so is its
whole-region-flush variant, \REtwentyMRegions. Removing the clock, so that a flush fires when the
agent writes, is fully distinguished, \REtwentyMOnDemand --- the occurrence of a flush is the
deduplication bit. Placing by content under a clock is distinguished too, \REtwentyMContent, because
the region a reactive write lands in tracks the retrieved fact. And Mnemos-P, dual-epoch secure by
\cref{thm:main}, is fully distinguished in the reactive game, \REtwentyPShipped: it flushes on demand
\emph{and} places by content. The game therefore separates the memory-resident design from every
ablation and from the partitioned design --- the empirical content of the $\mathrm{RDE}\Rightarrow
\mathrm{DE}$, $\mathrm{DE}\not\Rightarrow\mathrm{RDE}$ separation, and the reason the positive result
for Mnemos-M is \cref{thm:reactive} rather than a row in \cref{tab:e5}.

\subsection{The Byte-Level Auditor}
\label{sec:eval-audit}

An auditor snapshots the store around every operation of both sealed engines, diffs it byte by
byte, and requires each changed range to be named by an emitted event and each event to match a
real change. It confirms for Mnemos-P that a flush rewrites exactly $w+1$ whole regions whatever it
carries, that searches, sub-batch puts, deletes and updates write nothing until the next flush,
that a refused put still rewrites its region, and that closing re-encrypts every region; and for
Mnemos-M that a search, with or without its partition index, emits \emph{nothing at all}, and that
flushed regions stay uniform when every record has identical content. Under the ablations each
check fails exactly when its mechanism is removed. It also found a defect no functional test can:
rewrites owed to refused writes were held in a second queue drained after accepted records, so a
refusal-heavy history built a backlog whose length was itself a distinguisher. A mechanism correct
per operation can still leak across operations, which is why leakage is tested against the bytes
an adversary sees.

\subsection{Channels That Reach the Earlier Cohort}
\label{sec:eval-cohorth}
\label{sec:eval-write}
\label{sec:eval-multisnapshot}
\label{sec:eval-refusal}

\Cref{thm:main} closes the snapshot channel, and the game confirms it. Three channels reach cohort
H anyway, and each has a different answer.

\paragraph{The write channel}
Frequency analysis over induced writes labels regions with the topics of \emph{post-compromise}
writes. Against a growing layout those labels transfer to the earlier cohort, because the snapshot
discloses which region holds each stored record. Against a sealed fixed-size layout they do not,
because it discloses no such thing; this is the premise of \cref{lem:transfer}, and it is why an
advantage reported ``on the pre-compromise cohort'' against a sealed engine describes a harness
that supplied cohort-H membership rather than an adversary that had it.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_budget.pdf}
\caption{Exposure of the pre-compromise cohort against the adversary's induction budget, on a
timestamped stream compromised at a month boundary, for the write observer (left) and the
read-write observer (right); bands are 95\% intervals over twenty trials. The growing layout's
snapshot discloses which region holds each earlier record. For the sealed designs the harness
supplies that membership, so their curves are what the labels would be worth to an adversary that
obtained it elsewhere, for instance from version diffs. Mnemos-RS is measured for the write
observer, since its read-side channel is result-dependent reads. The supplementary material
tabulates every point.}
\label{fig:budget}
\end{figure*}

\Cref{fig:budget} reports the write channel as what it is. The distinction between its curves is
the lemma's third hypothesis made explicit: the growing layout's snapshot discloses which region
holds each earlier record, while for the sealed designs the harness supplies it, so those curves
state what the labels would be worth to an adversary that obtained membership elsewhere, for
instance from version diffs. It is also where the two
adversary classes separate. The read-observing adversary against Mnemos-P recovers the keyed
permutation from probe co-occurrence at the rate \cref{sec:eval-permutation} measures and labels
partitions from public text alone, with no induced writes; the write-observing one does neither.
Against Mnemos-M neither class learns anything, because a region's label carries no topic and no
search reaches the disk.

\paragraph{Multiple versions}
Version diffs are the channel that does supply cohort-H membership, and they refute the common
assumption that history independence makes several snapshots no worse than one. The region holding a
pre-$\tau$ record is inside the version diff at every granularity tested; what changes with
granularity is how precisely it is named. At the coarsest capture the diff covers the whole file
and the precision falls to the $1/B$ null, which is why capture granularity is a parameter of the
threat model rather than an assumption. Re-encrypting the whole file at a session boundary makes a
cross-session diff total, and drives the precision to exactly the null. \Cref{tab:ledger} gives the
channel in bits at each granularity; the full table is in the supplementary material.

\paragraph{Refusals}
An adversary that induces writes destined for a region and watches whether the region is rewritten
measures \cref{prop:capacity} directly. A refusal that writes nothing is not a small leak: a full
region is rewritten on \REnineSilentFull{} of induced writes against \REnineSilentOpen{} for an
open one, an AUC of \REnineSilentAUC, so the adversary learns whether a region has reached capacity
and therefore what the earlier cohort contributed to its load. Making a refused write perform the
rewrites an accepted one would have closes it, \REninePaddedAUC. The capacity arm is why this
matters in deployment rather than only in the game: under a skewed corpus the partitioned design
refuses its first write at \REnineUtilP\% of nominal capacity, so the admissible regime that
\cref{thm:main} assumes is left early, while Mnemos-M, with only a global bound, reaches
\REnineUtilM\%. The supplementary material tabulates both arms.

\subsection{Read-Side Channels, for the RW Class Only}
\label{sec:eval-rw}
\label{sec:eval-search}
\label{sec:eval-query}
\label{sec:eval-permutation}

The results in this subsection apply to the read-write observer and not to the write observer of
the anchoring instantiations, which sees no reads at all. The separation is worth stating
explicitly: a search-side leakage result that does not name its adversary class overstates what a
synchronization or imaging adversary learns.

\paragraph{Search and deletion}
The record-store design issues \RSearchReadsRS{} result-dependent reads over a search trace,
attaching a query topic to \RSearchPositionsRS{} record positions, many of them cohort H's, and
names the region of a deleted cohort-H record immediately in \RDeleteNamedRS{} of \RDeleteN{}
deletions. The partitioned design issues \RSearchReadsP{} such reads and names \RDeleteNamedP{}
regions, since it reads whole regions and defers deletion to the next flush; Mnemos-M's search does
not touch the disk at all.

\paragraph{Query equality}
Neither probe mechanism hides query equality from a determined adversary. Over
\REfifteenRepeats{} independent stores, with the adversary's overlap threshold fixed on one half
of the queries and scored on the other, the shipped randomized superset defeats a test for exact
equality of read sets (Youden's $J = \REfifteenExactStwo$) but not a threshold on their overlap,
$J = \REfifteenThreshStwo$, because repeated searches share every genuine probe and fresh cover
rarely collides. Doubling the cover to $s = 4$ lowers it to $J = \REfifteenThreshSfour$, at twice
the reads, without closing it. The grouped
mechanism leaks pairwise equality at group granularity, $J = \REfifteenThreshGrouped$, with a
deterministic anonymity set equal to the group size. Closing this channel requires oblivious
access, which \cref{sec:eval-perf} prices; the supplementary material gives every mechanism and
the anonymity-set analysis.

\paragraph{Permutation recovery}
Permutation recovery is the result that changes what the keyed permutation is for. Genuine probes are the
nearest partitions to a query, so the regions read together are geometric neighbours, and matching
the observed co-occurrence graph against the one the public table predicts recovers the permutation
from ordinary search traffic with no induced writes at all. Without probe randomization, a fast
approximate quadratic-assignment solver refined by pairwise swaps places a fraction
\REsevenExactCISone{} of the $B$ regions exactly over \REsevenRepeats{} independent stores (the
solver alone reaches \REsevenFaqSone, so the attack's strength is a property of the matcher and
the number is a lower bound). The shipped randomized superset degrades exact recovery to
\REsevenExactStwo;
grouped probing defeats it, \REsevenExactGrouped{} against a $1/B$ null of 0.016. This
reverses the order of preference between the two probe mechanisms, and it means the permutation
protects against a write observer holding a public table, not against a read observer. \Cref{tab:ledger}
gives the recovery in bits; the full table is in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Cost}
\label{sec:eval-perf}

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/fig_crossover.pdf}
\caption{Where the choice between the designs is decided. Three measures on different scales are
shown as small multiples over a shared $n$ rather than on shared axes. Mnemos-M's exhaustive
ranking is linear in $n$ and crosses the partitioned design between \REtenCrossLo{} and
\REtenCrossHi{} records; with its partition index held in memory (hollow markers) it stays ahead
at every size, paid for at unlock, where every record's partition is recomputed. What bounds
Mnemos-M is the middle panel, the memory its resident index occupies, which the index leaves
unchanged and which is therefore drawn once.}
\label{fig:crossover}
\end{figure*}

\Cref{fig:crossover} measures the three designs against the in-tree Rust plaintext IVF, which
shares \texttt{QuantVec} and the partitioner with the encrypted engines. The choice matters: a
numpy baseline whose insert rebuilds its whole array makes the plaintext write path slower than
the encrypted one and the ratio meaningless. Five sizes are measured, and writes are timed in the
sparse regime, because an agent writing seconds apart pays a whole flush per record; the
supplementary material tabulates every size, including bytes written per record.

The sweep answers the question \cref{sec:mnemos-m} raises, and not in the way a single size
would. Exhaustive in-memory ranking is \REtenSpeedupSmall{} times faster than the partitioned
design at \REtenNSmall{} records and \REtenSpeedupLarge{} times as fast at \REtenNLarge, because it
is linear in $n$ and the partitioned design is not: a store measured only at small $n$ would have
reported a speed advantage that inverts at scale. Probing logical partitions in memory removes
the inversion without reopening the write channel. At matched work, eight partitions scored per
query, Mnemos-M with its in-memory index is \REtenIvfSpeedupMin{} to \REtenIvfSpeedupMax{} times
faster than Mnemos-P across the sweep, \REtenIvfSearchLarge~ms against \REtenPSearchLarge~ms at
the largest size, because it reads and decrypts nothing. Its price is paid once, at unlock, where
every record's partition is recomputed: \REtenUnlockIvfLarge~s against \REtenUnlockMLarge~s at
\REtenNLarge{} records. A sparse write is also cheaper,
\REtenPutMLarge~ms against \REtenPutPLarge~ms there. The limit is resident memory,
\REtenResidentLarge~MB at \REtenNLarge{} records. Where that exceeds what a deployment will lock
is where the partitioned design becomes the right tool, with the residual channels of
\cref{sec:eval-cohorth,sec:eval-rw} stated.

The supplementary material prices the alternatives for fetching results once reads are visible. Reading by
position is cheapest and re-opens the result channel; reading everything leaks nothing and costs
$O(n)$; Path ORAM's cost is given as a model with its parameters stated rather than measured, since
there is no ORAM in the tree and a poor reimplementation would be worse than an honest formula. The
memory-resident design reaches the same leakage as the oblivious options at zero read cost, bounded
instead by memory, which is the substance of the design-space argument.

\paragraph{What the clocked flush writes}
The memory-resident design's flush is on a clock, so its cost is paid whether or not the agent
wrote, and a reader rightly asked what that costs on disk and on the drive. The submitted design
rewrote $w+1$ whole regions every $\Delta$, which at the shipped geometry is \REtwentyoneRegionMiB~MiB
a flush: at a $500$~ms clock an idle store writes \REtwentyoneRegionGBDay~GB a day, which is a
non-starter. We replaced it with a deterministic sweep, in the manner of write-only
ORAM~\cite{roche2017detwooram,aviv2017oblivisync,blass2014hive}: each flush rewrites the next fixed run of
slots after a cursor, plus a fixed-size sealed journal that holds staged records until the cursor
reaches a free slot, so the write pattern is a function of the flush index alone. A flush now writes
\REtwentyoneSweepKiB~KiB regardless of what was staged --- the byte count is identical for an empty
flush and a full one, which is the occurrence-hiding property stated as a measurement --- and at a
$\Delta=10$~s clock the store writes \REtwentyoneSweepGBDay~GB a day, roughly
\REtwentyoneRatio${\times}$ less than the region flush and about \REtwentyoneDriveYears{} years of
continuous operation against a $600$~TBW endurance rating. The byte-level auditor confirms that a
sweep flush writes exactly the cursor's slot run and the journal, and that the two are identical
whether or not a record was carried. Locking re-encrypts nothing extra for this design; for the
partitioned design a lock rewrites the whole partition file, \REtwentyonePLockMiB~MiB at the shipped
geometry, which a synchronization client uploads in full each session.

\paragraph{Repositioning the partitioned design}
The submission argued that the partitioned store is the right tool above the memory limit. Against
the write-only (class W) adversary that anchors the threat model, that is not quite right, and
write-only ORAM is why. The sweep already keeps a logical partition index in memory while placing
uniformly on disk (\cref{sec:mnemos-m}); pushing the headers to disk as well
(Mnemos-M/disk, \cref{sec:mnemos-m}) gives a store larger than memory whose \emph{writes} are still a
clock-driven sweep and therefore still invisible to W, closing the write, refusal, multi-version and
derived-write channels against W at scale. Only a \emph{read}-observing (RW) adversary sees its
access pattern, and only full obliviousness closes that. So the honest split is by adversary class:
for W, a write-only-oblivious store dominates the partitioned design; for RW, the open problem is
oblivious \emph{search}, whose cost we price against Path ORAM and, in \cref{sec:background}, against the
recent disk-oblivious ANN and private-memory systems~\cite{rathee2026onyx,kaviani2026opal,zhu2025compass}. The
partitioned design remains the point of comparison and the construction the dual-epoch theorem is
proved for, not the recommended deployment for W.

\subsection{Retrieval Quality}
\label{sec:eval-recall}

Retrieval is measured against BEIR relevance judgments on the \emph{full} corpora, and three
choices behind the measurement are worth stating because each one, made differently, inflates the
result. Truncating a corpus to 5{,}000 documents, under a tenth of FiQA's \REelevenFiqaSize, lifts
plaintext nDCG@10 far above the \REelevenFiqa{} these embeddings reach on the whole corpus.
Comparing against exhaustive plaintext search rather than an IVF at the same probe width folds
separate effects into one gap. And discarding capacity refusals during indexing hides relevant
documents that were never stored; here there were \REelevenRefusals.

Measured separately, the effects are small. Quantisation to int8, the distance from exhaustive
plaintext to Mnemos-M's exact search, costs at most \REelevenWorstQuant{} nDCG@10 on any domain;
fixed probing, the distance to a matched-probe plaintext IVF, costs up to \REelevenProbeCostMax.
Mnemos-M's in-memory index, scoring the same eight partitions per query as Mnemos-P at $s = 2$,
gains \REelevenIvfGainMin{} to \REelevenIvfGainMax{} nDCG@10 over it on every domain: all eight of
its partitions are the query's nearest, where half of Mnemos-P's are cover read to hide query
equality, and in memory that cover is unnecessary. The supplementary material tabulates every
domain and probe mechanism.

\subsection{Operating-System Artifacts}
\label{sec:eval-artifacts}

\Cref{sec:artifacts} argues that a real system undercuts the $\tau$-arrival model's premise that
the adversary observes nothing before $\tau$, and the audit is run rather than asserted. On the

\subsection{Where the Information Goes}
\label{sec:eval-ledger}

\input{tables/ledger}

Each experiment reports its channel in its own unit, an AUC, an advantage, a precision against a
$1/B$ null, which makes the design space hard to read on one axis. \Cref{tab:ledger} converts every
one by Fano's inequality into a lower bound on the mutual information between the secret and the
adversary's view, per observation of the unit the channel concerns. The conversion needs only an
accuracy, so every channel is converted by one rule, and it is a lower bound, the only kind of
statement a measurement can make about leakage.

The open channels are not marginal: at a spread of one, permutation recovery carries at least
\RLedgerPermBits{} of the $\log_2 B = \RLedgerPermBitsMax$ bits of each region's partition, and
grouped probing takes it to zero; the multi-version channel depends on capture granularity and
vanishes across a session once the file is re-encrypted at lock. For every closed row the
informative number is the last column, the smallest effect the experiment had 80\% power to
detect: a closed channel is a bounded statement about this attacker at this sample size, not a
certificate.
