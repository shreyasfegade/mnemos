\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\IEEEPARstart{L}{arge} language model agents increasingly keep a persistent memory. While
triaging tickets, reading documents, or debugging code, an agent records what it has learned and
later retrieves relevant records by nearest-neighbor search over text embeddings
\cite{packer2023memgpt}. The records are written continuously and without human review, and the
memory is often stored on the user's own device. Over time it becomes a detailed account of the
work the agent has performed.

Security research on agent memory has so far concentrated on integrity. Poisoning attacks such
as MINJA \cite{dong2025minja}, AgentPoison \cite{chen2024agentpoison}, and MemoryGraft
\cite{srivastava2025memorygraft} insert records that alter the agent's later behavior, and the
OWASP LLM Top 10 lists vector and embedding weaknesses as a distinct risk
\cite{owasp2025llm08}. Confidentiality has received less attention. MEXTRA \cite{mextra2025}
extracts memory records through the agent's own prompting interface, and membership inference
against chat-agent memory likewise queries the running agent \cite{mrmmia2026}, but no existing
work models an adversary that obtains the storage layer. Encrypting the memory is the natural first
measure, because embeddings can be inverted to recover much of the original text
\cite{morris2023text}.

This paper studies an adversary that obtains the storage layer partway through the life of such a
store, for example through a compromised synchronization or backup account, or by imaging the
device more than once. From the moment of compromise, $\tau$, it observes storage activity and can
influence the agent's inputs; for entries written before $\tau$ it holds only stored state. Those
entries might be expected to be safer for having been written before the breach. Our thesis is that
this expectation is a security property in its own right --- \emph{historical confidentiality}, the
analogue for stored structure of what forward secrecy is for keys --- and that it is not delivered by
encryption, by snapshot security, or by history independence. Whether a given store provides it turns
on a precondition we make precise, and closing the obvious channel that supplies that precondition is
not enough, because storage, versioning, capacity and the agent's own control flow supply it by other
routes that compose across the two epochs.

\paragraph{The attack, and its precondition}
The attack rests on two properties of the setting. An agent's writes can be induced: an adversary
that supplies a document, a ticket, or an email causes the agent to write a record about it, and
that record is ordinary agent output. And encrypted vector indexes partition their records by
content, so repeated induced writes on a chosen topic reveal which partitions receive records on
that topic. Because partitions are assigned by content rather than by time, the resulting
partition-to-topic map describes the earlier records too. A transfer lemma makes this exact, and
shows the two cohorts are exposed equally.

The lemma has a third hypothesis, and it carries most of the weight: the adversary must learn
\emph{which partition holds a given earlier record}. A layout whose regions grow with occupancy
discloses that in the snapshot; one of fixed-size sealed regions does not. The paper is largely an
account of how an adversary obtains that membership anyway.

\paragraph{What actually protects, and what does not}
Two natural intuitions about this setting need qualifying. The first is that subject drift protects
early records, since the agent has moved on to other topics. It lowers the attack's advantage only
against an adversary that induces with current content; one that induces with archival material ---
old documents on the earlier subject --- recovers the advantage entirely. The second concerns history
independence. History independence is a single-snapshot notion \cite{naor2001anti}: it relates one
representation to the operations that produced it. It is sometimes read as also bounding what a
\emph{sequence} of snapshots reveals, which it does not, and snapshot-oblivious RAM exists precisely
because multiple snapshots differ from one \cite{du2022snapshot}. For these content-placed layouts
two retained versions diff to exactly the regions written between them, which is the write channel
applied before $\tau$.

Two further channels reach the earlier cohort without touching the layout. A store that bounds
per-partition capacity and refuses an overflowing write discloses, at near-perfect accuracy, that a
topic's load has reached capacity, and therefore what the earlier cohort contributed to it. And an
agent that acts on what it recalls leaks through its own operations; the weakest form of this, the
occurrence of a write, is enough, because a memory pipeline that skips a fact it already holds turns
deduplication into a membership oracle for facts written before the compromise.

\paragraph{A design space, not a construction}
The threat model already assumes the adversary cannot read the unlocked process's memory, and in
the local setting there is no remote server that must hold the index. Encrypted on-disk search is
therefore a choice rather than a requirement, and taking the assumption at face value yields a
design whose disk layout carries no content at all: placement is uniform over free slots, search
runs entirely in locked memory, and flushes happen on a clock in the manner of write-only ORAM
rather than on writes. That design closes all three channels above, and a composition theorem shows
it stays closed even when the writer is the self-writing agent itself. Its limit is resident memory,
and a partition index kept in locked memory rather than expressed in the disk layout gives it
sublinear search at no cost in leakage; pushing only the headers to disk extends it past the memory
limit while keeping its writes invisible to a write observer. What actually needs on-disk oblivious
\emph{search} is the read-observing adversary alone, and there our comparison is the priced ORAM cost
rather than a claim of closure. The partitioned design remains the point of comparison and the
construction the dual-epoch theorem is proved for, with its residual channels reported, including one
that is often misread as a structural leak: its keyed partition permutation is largely recovered from ordinary
search traffic by an adversary that observes reads.

The contributions are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Historical confidentiality as a security property, and its threat model.} We identify
the confidentiality of pre-compromise agent memory as a distinct system-level property, and give the
$\tau$-arrival adversary that defines it: split into a write-observing (W) and a read-write (RW)
class, anchored on synchronization, backup and imaging instantiations, parameterized by
version-capture granularity, and audited against the artifacts a real operating system retains
(\cref{sec:threat,sec:eval-artifacts}).
\item \textbf{Structure transfer, and the channels that supply its precondition.} The transfer
attack and a lemma stating the membership condition under which it reaches the earlier cohort;
evaluated with held-out estimators, against deployed stores under file-level encryption with
time-placed controls, and on records a language-model agent actually wrote. Three further channels
supply that condition when the layout withholds it --- multi-version reconstruction, the refusal
channel with a capacity--confidentiality proposition, and derived writes, the last instantiated as a
deduplication membership oracle against real agent-memory pipelines
(\cref{sec:transfer,sec:attack-refusal,sec:attack-derived,sec:attack-multisnapshot,sec:eval}).
\item \textbf{Dual-epoch security, and its place among existing notions.} An adaptive,
leakage-parameterized definition with leakage over plaintext operations; sufficient conditions that
cover capacity overflow; separations from forward/backward privacy, breach resistance and history
independence; and a reactive variant with a composition theorem proving the memory-resident design
secure against the self-writing agent, where a standard adaptive definition cannot
(\cref{sec:dualepoch,sec:eval-game,sec:eval-reactive}).
\item \textbf{A design space, implemented and measured on one axis.} Three designs spanning the
space, a byte-level auditor that checks every operation against the bytes it writes and found a
cross-operation defect no functional test could, and a leakage ledger that renders every channel and
every null in bits so the space reads on one axis. Two building results generalize: whole-region
rewrites are needed only when intra-region placement is deterministic (\cref{lem:slot}), and a
partition index costs nothing in leakage once it is logical rather than physical
(\cref{sec:mnemos,sec:mnemos-m,sec:eval-audit,sec:eval-ledger}).
\end{enumerate}
