\section{Dual-Epoch Security}
\label{sec:dualepoch}

The attack of \cref{sec:transfer} exposes cohort H almost as much as cohort P. This section
explains why, defines the notion that separates the two cohorts, gives sufficient conditions for
it, places it against existing notions, and extends it to an agent whose writes depend on what it
retrieves.

\subsection{A Transfer Lemma}
\label{sec:transfer-lemma}

The finding does not depend on a particular attack; it follows from the placement rule. The lemma
is at bottom an exchangeability observation, and its content is in the \emph{third} hypothesis. It
states structure transfer as a property of \emph{composed} leakage: two leakages that are each
independent of cohort-H topics, the partition of each stored record and the labels induced writes
attach to partitions, together determine those topics.

\begin{lemma}[Transfer]
\label{lem:transfer}
Let records be assigned to partitions by a function of content alone, let cohorts H and P be
drawn from the same distribution over content, and let the adversary observe the partition of
each stored record. For any partition-labeling $\ell$ that the adversary computes without
reference to cohort membership, the expected topic-recovery accuracy of $\ell$ on cohort H equals
its expected accuracy on cohort P.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
A record's partition, and hence its predicted label, depends only on its content, as does its true
topic. Whether $\ell$ is correct on a record is therefore a function of content, whose distribution
is the same in both cohorts.
\end{proof}

Applying a partition label to a cohort-H record requires knowing which partition holds it. A
growing layout discloses that in the snapshot; against fixed-size sealed regions the lemma says
nothing. The write channel of \cref{sec:eval-write} is therefore a statement about cohort P unless
another channel supplies cohort-H membership, and \cref{sec:eval-multisnapshot} tests one. Subject
drift breaks the second hypothesis, which \cref{sec:eval-drift} measures.

\subsection{The Security Game}

Dual-epoch security is defined for a leakage function
$\mathcal{L} = (\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}, \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}})$: the state exposed at
$\tau$, and what each post-$\tau$ operation reveals. The leakage is stated over \emph{plaintext
operations}, as is usual for structured encryption; stating it as ``the regions the operation
touches'' would let the transcript reveal itself and make the theorem empty. Let $\pi$ be a secret
relabeling of partitions, sampled once. Then $\mathsf{put}(x)$ reveals $\pi(\mathrm{part}(x))$;
$\mathsf{search}(q)$ reveals $\pi$ of the probe set of $q$ and the fresh random extras of that call;
$\mathsf{delete}$ and $\mathsf{update}$ reveal nothing when called, and an update reveals the new
record's relabeled partition at its next flush; every operation reveals its time. The simulator
samples $\pi$ itself, so the keyed permutation buys consistency of the relabeling and nothing more
(\cref{sec:eval-permutation} measures what that is worth against a read observer).

The game $\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{H}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}$ for a dynamic encrypted memory $\Pi$ runs as
follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal{A}$ outputs two histories $\sigma_0, \sigma_1$ of equal length, each a sequence of
$\mathsf{put}$ and $\mathsf{delete}$ operations. Either may drive a partition past capacity.
\item The challenger samples $b \leftarrow \{0,1\}$, initializes $\Pi$, executes $\sigma_b$, and
flushes, so no record of $\sigma_b$ remains staged. This point is $\tau$. $\mathcal{A}$ receives
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$.
\item $\mathcal{A}$ issues $\mathsf{put}$, $\mathsf{delete}$, $\mathsf{update}$ and $\mathsf{search}$
operations \emph{adaptively}, each chosen after the leakage of the earlier ones, and may reference
records of $\sigma_b$. After each it receives $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$, never a search result.
\item $\mathcal{A}$ outputs $b'$; its advantage is
$\AdvH_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) = \bigl|\Pr[b'=b] - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr|$.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{definition}[$\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch security]
\label{def:dualepoch}
$\Pi$ is $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure if for every probabilistic polynomial-time $\mathcal{A}$
there is a simulator $\mathcal{S}$ such that the view of $\mathcal{A}$ in
$\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{H}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}$ is computationally indistinguishable from the output of
$\mathcal{S}$ given only $\mathcal{L}$ of the operations $\mathcal{A}$ issued; consequently
$\AdvH_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) \le \negl(\lambda)$.
\end{definition}

The simulator sees nothing about $\sigma_b$, so the entire view is independent of which history
produced cohort H. The flush at $\tau$ matters because a staged record would otherwise be written in
the adversary's view; equal lengths matter because the maintenance cursor and global occupancy
count pre-$\tau$ operations. Adaptivity matters because the $\tau$-arrival adversary induces writes
in response to what it has seen; the hybrid proof is unaffected, since each step replaces a
primitive rather than fixing the operation sequence.

\begin{corollary}[Predicate hiding]
\label{cor:predicate}
If $\Pi$ is $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure, then for any predicate $\phi$ on cohort-H contents,
in particular the topic of any record, no adversary predicts $\phi$ from its view with advantage
over the best cohort-oblivious predictor exceeding $2\,\AdvH_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)$, averaged
over the distribution from which the histories are drawn.
\end{corollary}

The argument is the usual equivalence of semantic security and indistinguishability (supplementary
material). A measured advantage is a \emph{lower} bound on what one classifier extracts, so
\cref{sec:eval-game} reports the upper end of each interval as the ceiling for the attacker it ran
and claims nothing beyond it.

\subsection{Sufficient Conditions}
\label{sec:conditions}

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:main}
Let $\Pi$ store records in $B$ partitions of fixed capacity $C$, in a single file of $B$ equal
regions, and let it satisfy the following conditions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S1 (fixed size).} Every file the store maintains has a length determined by the public
parameters alone.
\item \textbf{S2 (sealed slots).} Every slot is encrypted with an authenticated cipher that is
IND\$-CPA and INT-CTXT, under a fresh $192$-bit random nonce; a free slot is uniformly random bytes;
and any cleartext index within a slot is the output of a pseudorandom function of a uniformly random
identifier.
\item \textbf{S3 (data-independent updates, with padded refusal).} Each flush rewrites exactly $w+1$
whole regions: one per staged record, uniformly random regions for the remainder up to $w$, and one
region chosen by a counter that depends only on the flush index. A deletion writes nothing until the
next flush. \emph{A write whose partition is full is refused to the caller but performs the same
region rewrite an accepted one would have.} The manifest is rewritten every flush regardless of
content.
\item \textbf{S4 (independent placement).} The partition table is independent of cohort H.
\end{itemize}
Then $\Pi$ is $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure for the $\mathcal{L}$ above, with
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$ empty beyond the public parameters, for every pair of equal-length
histories, including pairs that overflow a partition.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
$\mathbf{G}_1$ replaces every pseudorandom function with a random one; $\mathbf{G}_2$ replaces
every ciphertext, live or free, with a uniform string of the same length (IND\$-CPA; S1 makes the
lengths match). In $\mathbf{G}_2$ the snapshot is fixed-length uniform strings, independent of $b$.
Each post-$\tau$ flush writes the regions S3 names: the staged records' relabeled partitions, uniform
choices and a counter, none of which depends on the history behind the records already present, and
by S4 neither does the table. A write refused under one history and accepted under the other
performs the same rewrite in both, so overflow does not distinguish. The view is a function of
$\mathcal{L}$ and public randomness. The full proof, with the concrete bound, is in the supplementary
material.
\end{proof}

Padded refusal is what lets the theorem cover overflow: a store that rewrites the intended region
whether or not the write was admitted makes the two observationally identical. Overflow then costs
availability, not confidentiality, since the agent's write is discarded; \cref{sec:eval-refusal}
measures how many writes a skewed trace loses, and that the first refusal arrives at
\REnineUtilP\% of nominal capacity.

Whole-region rewrites make a write reveal which region changed and never which slot. A per-slot
write need not reveal occupancy either, if the slot is chosen well.

\begin{lemma}[Slot-index independence]
\label{lem:slot}
Let each accepted record occupy a slot drawn uniformly from the free slots of its region, and let
nothing else in the store depend on slot labels within a region. For any pair $\sigma_0, \sigma_1$
that never overfills a partition and any region $r$, the sequence of slot indices at which post-$\tau$
records are placed in $r$ has the same distribution under $b = 0$ and $b = 1$.
\end{lemma}

The proof is exchangeability of slot labels (supplementary material). The lemma is as strong as its
hypothesis, which fails for maintenance: freeing tombstoned cohort-H records slot by slot writes as
many slots as were deleted, so the purge stays a whole-region rewrite. Nor does it hide
\emph{whether} a write happened, which a clocked flush must: a slot written twice with no deletion
between was padding the first time. \Cref{sec:eval-game} measures all four combinations of placement
rule and write granularity.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Notions}
\label{sec:relations}

\Cref{tab:notions} places dual-epoch security against existing notions, and the results below are
the separations; constructions and reductions are in the supplementary material. In one sentence,
dual-epoch security is \emph{storage forward secrecy for structure}: it bounds what a compromise at
$\tau$ reveals about the placement of data written before $\tau$.

\begin{proposition}[Forward and backward privacy are insufficient]
\label{prop:fp}
There is a scheme that is forward and Type-II backward private but not dual-epoch secure.
\end{proposition}
\noindent The witness adjoins a per-partition put counter to the client state of any such scheme.
Transcript notions are blind to it, but the snapshot's multiset of counters separates $m$ puts to one
partition from $m/2$ to each of two. Such state (Sophos's counters \cite{bost2016sophos}, Diana's
GGM keys \cite{bost2017forward}) leaks little on a realistic corpus (supplementary material); the
existence separation and its magnitude are separate claims.

\begin{proposition}[Breach resistance is implied, and insufficient]
\label{prop:breach}
Dual-epoch security with snapshot leakage $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$ implies breach resistance
with respect to $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$. The converse fails, even together with forward and
backward privacy.
\end{proposition}
\noindent Restricting a dual-epoch simulator to the snapshot gives a breach-resistance simulator; the
witness of \cref{prop:fp} separates the converse. Neither notion constrains the \emph{joint}
object, a snapshot read with the writes an adaptive adversary induces after it, and that object is
where structure transfer lives.

\begin{proposition}[History independence is insufficient]
\label{prop:hi}
A store that is strongly history-independent in the sense of Naor--Teague need not be dual-epoch
secure, even against a single post-$\tau$ snapshot.
\end{proposition}
\noindent History independence hides the order in which a set was inserted, not which set was
inserted; a content-placed HI store still keeps a topic in that topic's region.

\begin{proposition}[Sealing and padding are each necessary]
\label{prop:seal}
Slot-level history independence with regions that grow with occupancy is not dual-epoch secure,
and padding regions to a fixed size without sealing free slots is not dual-epoch secure.
\end{proposition}
\noindent The first leaks occupancy through region sizes, the second through the boundary between
written and unwritten slots; \cref{sec:eval-game} realizes both as ablations.

\paragraph{Version retention as a leakage parameter} The multi-version channel lies outside
\cref{def:dualepoch}, whose snapshot is one state. Let $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}_g$ additionally
reveal the file's versions every $g$ flushes before $\tau$; \emph{$g$-retained dual-epoch security}
is \cref{def:dualepoch} with $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}_g$. Security at $g=\infty$ does not imply it
at finite $g$: Mnemos-P meets \cref{thm:main}, yet its version diffs name a pre-$\tau$ record's
region (\cref{sec:eval-multisnapshot}). A clock-only store (\cref{thm:reactive}) is $g$-retained
secure for every $g$, since a diff at any granularity reveals only which cursor ranges were swept.
The same asymmetry decides what an imaging adversary gains from superseded flash pages.

\subsection{Capacity and Confidentiality}
\label{sec:capacity-prop}

\begin{proposition}[Capacity--confidentiality trade-off]
\label{prop:capacity}
Let $\Pi$ place records by content into partitions of capacity $C$, and let a write whose partition
is full be refused without performing the writes an accepted record would have performed. Then an
adversary that can induce writes on a topic and observe the operation leakage learns whether that
topic's live load has reached $C$, and hence the load contributed by cohort H. Dual-epoch security
therefore requires either that $C$ exceed the worst-case per-topic load, or that a refusal be
indistinguishable from an acceptance.
\end{proposition}

An accepted put reveals $\pi(\mathrm{part}(x))$ and a silently refused one reveals nothing, so the
distinguishing event is exactly ``this topic is at capacity.'' Padded refusal (S3) takes the second
horn and pays in lost writes; over-provisioning takes the first and pays in provisioning for the
most frequent topic, which under skew is most of the file. \Cref{sec:eval-refusal} measures both. A
design with only a \emph{global} bound, such as \cref{sec:mnemos-m}, escapes the proposition,
because global occupancy is a function of the record count, which the game equalizes.

\subsection{Reactive Security and the Derived-Write Channel}
\label{sec:derived-write}

In \cref{def:dualepoch} the adversary chooses every post-$\tau$ operation. A self-writing agent is a
second source of operations: it retrieves a cohort-H record and, in the same turn, writes or
searches about the same subject, so a post-$\tau$ operation is a function of cohort-H plaintext that
the storage adversary cannot read. An adaptive adversary does not have that secret-dependent control
flow, so this is a separate model: secure storage composed with a reactive program.

\paragraph{The reactive game} A \emph{feedback agent} $\mathsf{P}$ is a stateful polynomial-time
program that, on each turn, receives an input, may search $\Pi$ and read the plaintext results, and
then issues $\mathsf{put}$, $\mathsf{delete}$ or $\mathsf{update}$ operations. The game
$\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}$ runs as $\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{H}}$ through
$\tau$; then the challenger runs $\mathsf{P}$ on inputs the adversary supplies one at a time, and
after each turn the adversary receives $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$ of the operations $\mathsf{P}$
issued and nothing else: no output of $\mathsf{P}$, no search result. $\AdvR_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}$
is defined as before.

\begin{definition}[Reactive dual-epoch security]
\label{def:reactive}
$\Pi$ is \emph{reactively $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure} (RDE) if for every polynomial-time
feedback agent $\mathsf{P}$ and every polynomial-time $\mathcal{A}$ there is a simulator with the
view of $\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{R}}$, so that $\AdvR_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) \le
\negl(\lambda)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{proposition}[Derived-write separation]
\label{prop:derived}
Let $\Pi$ have post-$\tau$ operation leakage $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$ that reveals a non-constant
function of the content of an operation, or of whether an operation occurred at all. Then there is a
feedback agent $\mathsf{P}$ against which $\Pi$ is not RDE, even if $\Pi$ is dual-epoch secure by
\cref{def:dualepoch}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Let $\mathsf{P}$ be a deduplicating writer: on input $x$ it searches for $x$ and writes $x$ only if
the top result is not a near-duplicate. Let $\sigma_0,\sigma_1$ differ in whether a fact $x^\star$ is
present, and supply $x^\star$. $\mathsf{P}$ writes under one history and not the other, which
occurrence leakage reveals; if $\mathsf{P}$ always writes, choose histories under which its write
lands in different partitions, which content leakage reveals. Mnemos-P flushes on demand and places
by content, so it meets \cref{thm:main} and is not RDE.
\end{proof}

The hypothesis excludes exactly the stores whose disk behaviour is a function of the clock alone.
For those the composition goes through.

\begin{theorem}[Composition: a clock-only store is reactively secure]
\label{thm:reactive}
Let $\Pi$ satisfy S1 and S2 and, in place of S3--S4, the clock conditions
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 (clocked schedule).} The set of disk locations written at each flush is a function
of the flush index alone --- a deterministic sweep of $s$ slots per flush from a start fixed at
unlock --- and a flush occurs every $\Delta$ while unlocked whether or not anything was staged.
\item \textbf{C2 (fresh sealed writes).} Every written slot is a fresh authenticated ciphertext of
the fixed slot length, live or free alike.
\item \textbf{C3 (no post-unlock reads).} After the single sequential read at unlock, a
$\mathsf{search}$ issues no disk access; ranking and payload retrieval are served from the locked
arena.
\end{itemize}
Then for every feedback agent $\mathsf{P}$, $\Pi$ is reactively $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clock}}$-dual-epoch
secure, where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clock}}$ reveals only the unlock, lock and flush times.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The same two hybrids make the snapshot fixed-length uniform strings. Each flush then writes the
slot range C1's cursor names, a function of the flush index and not of anything $\mathsf{P}$ did,
wrote or read; by C3 no search adds a disk event. The view is the clock and uniform bytes, which a
simulator holding only $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clock}}$ reproduces without $\mathsf{P}$, its inputs, or
$b$. The supplementary material gives the full argument, including the sealed journal that carries
staged records between flushes on the same clock.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mnemos-M is reactively secure]
\label{cor:m-reactive}
The memory-resident store of \cref{sec:mnemos-m}, with its deterministic sweep flush, in-memory
search, and clocked schedule, satisfies C1--C3 and is reactively dual-epoch secure against every
feedback agent. Mnemos-P is not (\cref{prop:derived}), so
$\text{RDE} \Rightarrow \text{DE}$ and $\text{DE} \not\Rightarrow \text{RDE}$.
\end{corollary}

\Cref{sec:eval-reactive} plays the reactive game against both designs and a graded series of
ablations, and finds the clock-only store at chance while every store that flushes on a write or
places by content is distinguished. This is what separates self-writing memory from classical
searchable encryption, whose queries are external to the stored data: closing the channel needs
content-oblivious access \emph{and} a content-independent operation schedule.
