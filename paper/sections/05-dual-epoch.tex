\section{Dual-Epoch Security}
\label{sec:dualepoch}

The attack of \cref{sec:transfer} exposes cohort H almost as much as cohort P. This section
explains why, states the security notion that separates the two cohorts, and gives the conditions
under which the separation can be made to hold. Three results delimit what any storage design can
achieve: a transfer lemma showing that a content-placed layout exposes the two cohorts equally, a
capacity result showing that bounded per-partition capacity leaks the earlier cohort's load, and a
separation showing that a self-writing agent leaks cohort H through its own retrievals against any
store whose operations depend on content.

\subsection{A Transfer Lemma}
\label{sec:transfer-lemma}

The empirical finding that cohort H is exposed nearly as much as cohort P does not depend on a
particular attack; it follows from the placement rule. The lemma below is, at bottom, an
exchangeability observation --- its whole content is in its \emph{third} hypothesis --- but stating
it as a lemma makes the load-bearing precondition explicit and turns structure transfer into a
statement about \emph{composed} leakage: two leakages that are each independent of cohort-H topics,
the unlabeled partition of each stored record and the labels induced writes attach to partitions,
together determine those topics. That is precisely the joint object no snapshot-only or
transcript-only notion evaluates, and it is what \cref{def:dualepoch} is built to.

\begin{lemma}[Transfer]
\label{lem:transfer}
Let records be assigned to partitions by a function of content alone, let cohorts H and P be
drawn from the same distribution over content, and let the adversary observe the partition of
each stored record. For any partition-labeling $\ell$ that the adversary computes without
reference to cohort membership, the expected topic-recovery accuracy of $\ell$ on cohort H equals
its expected accuracy on cohort P.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Placement depends only on content, so a record's partition is independent of its cohort given its
content. The labeling $\ell$ maps partitions to topics without reading cohort membership, so the
predicted label of a record depends only on its content. The true topic is a function of content.
Hence the event that $\ell$ is correct on a record depends only on its content, whose distribution
is identical across cohorts; the expectations are therefore equal.
\end{proof}

The third hypothesis is easily overlooked. Applying a partition label to a cohort-H record
requires knowing which partition holds it: a growing layout discloses that in the snapshot, and
against a layout of fixed-size sealed regions the lemma says nothing at all. The write channel of
\cref{sec:eval-write} is therefore a statement about cohort P unless another channel supplies
cohort-H membership, and \cref{sec:eval-multisnapshot} exhibits one. The other hypotheses name the
ways the cohorts' exposure can differ: subject drift breaks identical distributions, which
\cref{sec:eval-drift} measures together with how much an adversary inducing with archival content
recovers. Dual-epoch security is thus valuable not because historical data is inherently safer,
but because a store can be built whose adversary-visible representation carries no partition
labels at all.

\subsection{The Security Game}

Dual-epoch security is defined with respect to a leakage function
$\mathcal{L} = (\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}, \mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}})$, where
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$ describes the state exposed at $\tau$ and $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$
describes each post-$\tau$ operation. Making the leakage explicit lets the definition cover the
operations a real agent performs, searches and deletions included.

The leakage is stated over \emph{plaintext operations}, in the style usual for structured
encryption, rather than over the transcript the store happens to produce. Writing it the other way,
as "the physical regions the operation reads or writes", makes the definition circular: the
transcript is then permitted to reveal itself, and the theorem says little. Let $\pi$ be a secret
relabeling of partitions, sampled once and used consistently. Then
\begin{itemize}
\item $\mathsf{put}(x)$ reveals $\pi(\mathrm{part}(x))$;
\item $\mathsf{search}(q)$ reveals $\pi$ applied to the probe set of $q$, together with the fresh
random extras drawn on that call;
\item $\mathsf{delete}$ and $\mathsf{update}$ reveal nothing at the time of the call, and an
update additionally reveals the new record's relabeled partition at its next flush;
\item every operation reveals the time at which it occurred.
\end{itemize}
The simulator samples $\pi$ itself, which is what makes explicit that the keyed permutation buys
consistency of the relabeling and nothing more. \Cref{sec:eval-permutation} measures how much even
that is worth against an adversary that observes reads.

Let $\Pi$ be a dynamic encrypted memory. The game $\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{H}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}$ is
played between a challenger and an adversary $\mathcal{A}$.
\begin{enumerate}
\item $\mathcal{A}$ outputs two histories $\sigma_0, \sigma_1$ of equal length, each a sequence of
$\mathsf{put}$ and $\mathsf{delete}$ operations. In the submission this step required the histories
to be \emph{admissible} --- no partition over capacity at any point. The revised theorem removes
that restriction (\cref{sec:conditions}): a store that rewrites the intended region whether or not
a write was admitted hides overflow, so the histories may drive any partition past capacity.
\item The challenger samples $b \leftarrow \{0,1\}$, initializes $\Pi$, executes $\sigma_b$, and
performs a flush, so that no record of $\sigma_b$ remains staged. This point is $\tau$. The
challenger gives $\mathcal{A}$ the snapshot leakage $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$.
\item $\mathcal{A}$ then issues operations \emph{adaptively}: having seen the leakage of every
earlier operation, it chooses the next one, from $\mathsf{put}$, $\mathsf{delete}$,
$\mathsf{update}$, and $\mathsf{search}$, and may reference records written in $\sigma_b$. After
each operation the challenger returns $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$.
\item $\mathcal{A}$ outputs $b' \in \{0,1\}$.
\end{enumerate}
The advantage is
$\AdvH_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) = \bigl|\Pr[b'=b] - \tfrac{1}{2}\bigr|$.

Adaptivity matters because the $\tau$-arrival adversary is inherently adaptive: it induces writes
in response to what it has already observed. The hybrid argument in the supplementary material is
unchanged by it, because each hybrid step replaces a primitive rather than fixing the operation
sequence.

\begin{definition}[$\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch security]
\label{def:dualepoch}
$\Pi$ is $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure if for every probabilistic polynomial-time $\mathcal{A}$
there is a simulator $\mathcal{S}$ such that the view $\mathcal{A}$ receives in
$\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{H}}_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}$ is computationally indistinguishable from the output of
$\mathcal{S}$ given only $\mathcal{L}$ evaluated on the operations $\mathcal{A}$ issued, and
consequently $\AdvH_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) \le \negl(\lambda)$.
\end{definition}

Because the simulator sees the post-$\tau$ leakage but nothing about $\sigma_b$, the definition
states that the entire view is independent of which history produced cohort H. The flush at $\tau$
matters: a record staged but unflushed at $\tau$ would be written in full view of the adversary.
Equal lengths matter too: the maintenance cursor's resting position counts pre-$\tau$ flushes, and
global occupancy bounds how many further records the store accepts, so unequal lengths would make
the definition unsatisfiable for reasons unrelated to placement (\cref{sec:mnemos-write} gives the
deployment measure for the cursor).

\Cref{def:dualepoch} concerns indistinguishability of histories, not topic recovery. The two are
connected by a standard reduction.

\begin{corollary}[Predicate hiding]
\label{cor:predicate}
If $\Pi$ is $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure, then for any predicate $\phi$ on cohort-H contents,
in particular the topic of any record, no adversary predicts $\phi$ from its view with advantage
over the best cohort-oblivious predictor exceeding $2\,\AdvH_{\Pi,\mathcal{A}}(\lambda)$, where the
advantage is averaged over the distribution from which the histories are drawn.
\end{corollary}

The argument is the usual equivalence between semantic security and indistinguishability, with
the averaging over histories that lets a $\phi$-predictor fix a distinguishing pair; the
supplementary material spells it out. The corollary must not be read backwards: a measured
advantage is a \emph{lower} bound on what one classifier extracts, so \cref{sec:eval-game} reports
the upper end of each interval as the ceiling for the attacker it ran, and claims nothing beyond
it.

\subsection{Sufficient Conditions}
\label{sec:conditions}

The submitted theorem carried a fourth condition, \emph{admissibility}: that neither history drive
any partition over its capacity at any point during the game. It was stated as necessary, on the
grounds that a scheme cannot hide whether a record overflowed a partition. That is true only of a
store that treats a refused write differently from an accepted one. A store that rewrites the
intended region \emph{whether or not} the write was admitted --- the padded refusal now folded into
S3 --- makes the two observationally identical, so the overflow is invisible. We therefore remove
admissibility from the theorem: \cref{thm:main} holds for every pair of equal-length histories,
including pairs that differ precisely in whether some partition overflows. Overflow costs not
confidentiality but availability, since a padded refusal discards the agent's write;
\cref{sec:eval-refusal} reports how many writes a realistic skewed trace loses and that the first
refusal arrives at \REnineUtilP\% of nominal capacity. Admissibility survives only as the
hypothesis of \cref{lem:slot}, where a per-slot write genuinely needs it.

\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:main}
Let $\Pi$ store records in $B$ partitions of fixed capacity $C$, in a single file of $B$ equal
regions, and let it satisfy the following conditions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{S1 (fixed size).} Every file the store maintains has a length determined by the public
parameters alone.
\item \textbf{S2 (sealed slots).} Every slot is encrypted with an authenticated cipher that is
IND\$-CPA and INT-CTXT, under a fresh $192$-bit random nonce; a free slot is uniformly random bytes;
and any cleartext index within a slot is the output of a pseudorandom function of a uniformly random
identifier.
\item \textbf{S3 (data-independent updates, with padded refusal).} Each flush rewrites exactly $w+1$
whole regions: one per staged record, uniformly random regions for the remainder up to $w$, and one
region chosen by a counter that depends only on the flush index. A deletion writes nothing until the
next flush. \emph{A write whose partition is full is refused to the caller but performs the same
region rewrite an accepted one would have.} The manifest is rewritten every flush regardless of
content.
\item \textbf{S4 (independent placement).} The partition table is independent of cohort H.
\end{itemize}
Then $\Pi$ is $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure for the $\mathcal{L}$ above, with
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$ empty beyond the public parameters, and the bound holds for every pair
of equal-length histories rather than only for admissible ones.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The proof is a sequence of hybrids over the game, given in full in the supplementary material.
$\mathbf{G}_0$ is the real game. $\mathbf{G}_1$ replaces every pseudorandom function with a random function; a
distinguisher breaks the PRF. $\mathbf{G}_2$ replaces every ciphertext, live or free, with a
uniformly random string of the same length; a distinguisher breaks IND\$-CPA, and S1 guarantees
the lengths match. In $\mathbf{G}_2$ the snapshot is a collection of fixed-length uniform strings
whose sizes are public parameters, so by S1 and S2 it is independent of $b$. Each post-$\tau$
operation writes a set of whole regions determined by S3, whose identities are the staged records'
relabeled partitions, uniform choices, and a content-independent counter, none of which depends on
which history produced the records already present. Under S4 the partition table is independent of
$b$. A write refused under one history but accepted under the other performs, by S3's padded
refusal, the identical region rewrite in both cases, so the refusal rule is exercised without
distinguishing --- which is what lets the theorem drop the admissibility restriction the submission
imposed. Hence the whole view is a function of $\mathcal{L}$ and public randomness, which the
simulator reproduces.
\end{proof}

Padding a flush with rewrites of uniformly random regions rather than with dummy records keeps the
write pattern data-independent without consuming capacity, so admissibility can be stated in terms
of real load alone.

Rewriting whole regions (S3) makes a write reveal which region changed and never which slot, and
it is natural to assume that a per-slot write would reveal how full the region was. It need not.
Whether the index of a written slot carries occupancy depends on how the slot was chosen, and a
uniformly random choice among the free slots makes whole-region rewrites unnecessary for placement.

\begin{lemma}[Slot-index independence]
\label{lem:slot}
Let each accepted record occupy a slot drawn uniformly from the free slots of its region, and let
nothing else in the store depend on slot labels within a region. For any admissible pair
$\sigma_0, \sigma_1$ and any region $r$, the sequence of slot indices at which post-$\tau$ records
are placed in $r$ has the same distribution under $b = 0$ and $b = 1$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The placement rule is invariant under any permutation of the slot labels of $r$, and by hypothesis
nothing else distinguishes them, so the labels are exchangeable. Hence the set $O_b(r)$ of slots
cohort H occupies at $\tau$ is a uniformly random subset of size $L_b(r)$, whatever the history.
Post-$\tau$ records draw uniformly without replacement from its complement, so $k$ of them occupy a
uniformly random ordered $k$-subset of the complement of a uniformly random $L_b(r)$-subset, which is
a uniformly random ordered $k$-subset of all $C$ labels whenever $k \le C - L_b(r)$. Admissibility
guarantees that bound under both histories, so the distribution does not depend on $b$. By S2 the
snapshot does not reveal $O_b(r)$, so the adversary cannot condition on it.
\end{proof}

The lemma is exactly as strong as its hypothesis, which fails for the maintenance step: freeing
tombstoned cohort-H records slot by slot writes as many slots as were deleted there, which depends
on the history, so the purge must remain a whole-region rewrite. Record placement need not;
\cref{sec:eval-game} measures all four combinations of placement rule and write granularity, and
only the lowest free slot, written alone, distinguishes the histories.

A second boundary is set by what the game reveals. Every post-$\tau$ put is in
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$, so the lemma says nothing about hiding \emph{whether} a write
happened, which Mnemos-M's clocked flush promises. There padding must look like placement, and slot
by slot it does not: a slot written twice with no deletion between was padding the first time, so
over enough flushes the adversary counts the puts. Per-slot placement is therefore available to the
partitioned design and not to the memory-resident one, where it would have saved writing $C$ slots
per record.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Notions}
\label{sec:relations}

Dual-epoch security is not a renaming of an existing notion, and the way to show that is to place it
against them and exhibit separations. \Cref{tab:notions} is the map; the results below are the
edges. The one-sentence positioning is that dual-epoch security is \emph{storage forward secrecy for
structure}: it bounds what a compromise at $\tau$ reveals about data written before $\tau$, exactly
as forward secrecy bounds what a key compromise reveals about earlier sessions, but for the
placement structure of a self-writing store rather than for a key.

\emph{and} the post-compromise transcript \emph{jointly}, across two cohorts, and its reactive form
adds the agent's own control flow.}
\label{tab:notions}

The conditions are not implied by the standard notions, and dual-epoch security is not implied by
composing them. We give the separations in both directions.

\begin{proposition}[Forward and backward privacy are insufficient]
\label{prop:fp}
There is a scheme that is forward and Type-II backward private but not dual-epoch secure.
\end{proposition}
\emph{Construction and reduction.} Take any forward- and Type-II-backward-private dynamic SSE scheme
and adjoin to its client state a per-partition update counter, incremented on each $\mathsf{put}$
and stored under a pseudorandom handle in the snapshot. Forward and backward privacy are transcript
notions and are unaffected by client-side state, so the augmented scheme still enjoys them. But the
snapshot now reveals a \emph{multiset} of counter values: writing $m$ records to one partition yields
$\{m\}$, while writing $m/2$ to each of two yields $\{m/2,m/2\}$, and no relabeling of partitions
maps one to the other. The dual-epoch distinguisher submits those two histories and reads the
multiset off the snapshot, so $\AdvH$ is non-negligible. \Cref{sec:attack-counting} reports how much
this particular state leaks on a realistic corpus, where it happens to be weak --- the existence
separation and its practical magnitude are separate claims.

\begin{proposition}[Breach resistance is insufficient, and is implied]
\label{prop:breach}
Dual-epoch security with snapshot leakage $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$ implies breach resistance
with respect to $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$. The converse fails: there is a scheme that is
breach-resistant and forward/backward private but not dual-epoch secure.
\end{proposition}
The forward direction is immediate: breach resistance asks that a single snapshot be simulatable
from $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$, which is the $\tau$-only restriction of \cref{def:dualepoch}, so a
dual-epoch simulator restricted to the snapshot is a breach-resistance simulator. The converse is
\cref{prop:fp}'s witness: breach resistance constrains one snapshot and forward/backward privacy the
transcript, but neither constrains the \emph{joint} object --- a snapshot read together with the
post-$\tau$ writes an adaptive adversary induces --- which is where structure transfer lives. This is
the sense in which dual-epoch security is the missing composition rather than a new primitive.

\begin{proposition}[History independence is insufficient]
\label{prop:hi}
A store that is strongly history-independent in the sense of Naor--Teague need not be dual-epoch
secure, even against a single post-$\tau$ snapshot.
\end{proposition}
History independence fixes the \emph{distribution} of the stored state given its contents, so two
insertion orders of the same set are indistinguishable. It says nothing about two different
\emph{sets} drawn from the two challenge histories, which is what \cref{def:dualepoch} distinguishes;
a content-placed HI store still puts a topic's records in that topic's region, and the growing-layout
attack reads the region off the snapshot. \Cref{prop:seal} makes this concrete.

\begin{proposition}[Sealing and padding are each necessary]
\label{prop:seal}
Slot-level history independence with regions that grow with occupancy is not dual-epoch secure,
and padding regions to a fixed size without sealing free slots is not dual-epoch secure.
\end{proposition}
The first store leaks per-partition occupancy through region sizes even though within a region no
order is revealed; the second leaks occupancy through the boundary between written and unwritten
slots. \Cref{sec:eval-game} realizes both as ablations and measures the resulting advantage.

\paragraph{Version retention as a leakage parameter} The multi-version channel of
\cref{sec:artifacts} lives outside \cref{def:dualepoch}, whose snapshot is a single state. We bring
it inside by parameterizing the snapshot leakage with a capture granularity $g$: let
$\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}_g$ additionally reveal the store's file versions every $g$ flushes before
$\tau$. \emph{$g$-retained dual-epoch security} is \cref{def:dualepoch} with $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}_g$
in place of $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{snap}}$. A store secure at $g=\infty$ (one snapshot) need not be
secure at finite $g$: Mnemos-P is dual-epoch secure by \cref{thm:main} but its between-version diffs
name a pre-$\tau$ record's region, so it fails $g$-retained security for small $g$
(\cref{sec:eval-multisnapshot}). A clock-only store (\cref{thm:reactive}) is $g$-retained secure for
\emph{every} $g$, because its writes are a function of the clock and a version diff at any
granularity reveals only which cursor ranges were swept. The same asymmetry is why an imaging
adversary that recovers superseded flash pages obtains Mnemos-P's version history --- whole-region
rewrites land at fixed offsets --- but obtains nothing structural from the sweep.

\subsection{Capacity and Confidentiality}
\label{sec:capacity-prop}

\Cref{thm:main} covers overflow because S3 mandates padded refusal. A store that instead refuses
\emph{silently} is not covered, and the reason is a genuine trade-off rather than an oversight.

\begin{proposition}[Capacity--confidentiality trade-off]
\label{prop:capacity}
Let $\Pi$ place records by content into partitions of capacity $C$, and let a write whose partition
is full be refused without performing the writes an accepted record would have performed. Then an
adversary that can induce writes on a topic and observe the operation leakage learns whether that
topic's live load has reached $C$. Since the adversary knows its own contribution to that load, it
learns the load contributed by cohort H. Dual-epoch security therefore requires either that $C$
exceed the worst-case per-topic load, or that a refusal be indistinguishable from an acceptance.
\end{proposition}

The proof is immediate: an accepted put reveals $\pi(\mathrm{part}(x))$ and a silently refused
one reveals nothing, and the distinguishing event is exactly ``this topic's load is at capacity''.
The two disjuncts are the two horns. Padded refusal (S3, and the choice \cref{thm:main} makes)
takes the second: it hides the load, at the price of discarding the write, so the cost is
availability. Over-provisioning $C$ takes the first: it keeps every write, at the price of
provisioning for the most frequent topic, which under skew is most of the file. \Cref{sec:eval-refusal}
measures both --- the writes a padded store loses on a skewed trace, and the utilization at which a
silent store's channel opens. A design with only a \emph{global} capacity bound, such as
\cref{sec:mnemos-m}, escapes the proposition entirely, because global occupancy is a function of the
record count, which the game already equalizes; this is one of the two places the memory-resident
design is strictly easier to secure than the partitioned one.

\subsection{Reactive Security and the Derived-Write Channel}
\label{sec:derived-write}

\Cref{def:dualepoch} lets the adversary choose every post-$\tau$ operation. A self-writing agent is
a second, qualitatively different source of operations: it retrieves a cohort-H record and, in the
same turn, writes a new record or issues a follow-up query about the same subject, so an operation
performed after $\tau$ is a function of the plaintext of cohort~H --- data the storage adversary
cannot read. A reader rightly observed that folding this into \cref{def:dualepoch}, where the
adversary itself chooses operations, conflates two models: a standard adaptive adversary does not
have the secret-dependent control flow that makes an honest agent behave differently under the two
histories. The right object is a \emph{composition} statement about secure storage placed under a
reactive program. We give the model and prove both directions: no leaky store survives it, and a
clock-only store does.

\paragraph{The reactive game} A \emph{feedback agent} $\mathsf{P}$ is a stateful polynomial-time
program that, on each turn, receives an input, may issue $\mathsf{search}$ operations to $\Pi$ and
read their results, and then issues $\mathsf{put}$, $\mathsf{delete}$ or $\mathsf{update}$
operations. Crucially $\mathsf{P}$ sees plaintext search results; the adversary does not. The
reactive game $\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{R}}_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}$ runs as
$\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{H}}$ through $\tau$, and then, in place of the adversary's own operations, the
challenger runs $\mathsf{P}$ on a sequence of inputs the adversary supplies one at a time. After each
turn the adversary receives $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$ for the operations $\mathsf{P}$ issued --- and
nothing else: not $\mathsf{P}$'s outputs, not the search results $\mathsf{P}$ read. The advantage
$\AdvR_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}$ is defined as before.

\begin{definition}[Reactive dual-epoch security]
\label{def:reactive}
$\Pi$ is \emph{reactively $\mathcal{L}$-dual-epoch secure} (RDE) if for every polynomial-time
feedback agent $\mathsf{P}$ and every polynomial-time $\mathcal{A}$ there is a simulator with the
view of $\mathbf{G}^{\mathrm{R}}$, i.e. $\AdvR_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}(\lambda) \le
\negl(\lambda)$.
\end{definition}

That RDE is not implied by \cref{def:dualepoch} is the content of the derived-write attack, now
stated inside the model that makes it precise.

\begin{proposition}[Derived-write separation]
\label{prop:derived}
Let $\Pi$ have post-$\tau$ operation leakage $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$ that reveals a non-constant
function of the content of an operation, or of whether an operation occurred at all. Then there is a
feedback agent $\mathsf{P}$ against which $\Pi$ is not RDE, even if $\Pi$ is dual-epoch secure by
\cref{def:dualepoch}.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Take $\mathsf{P}$ to be a deduplicating writer: on input $x$ it searches for $x$, and writes $x$
only if the top result is not a near-duplicate. The adversary chooses $\sigma_0,\sigma_1$ to differ
in whether a particular fact $x^\star$ is present, and supplies $x^\star$ as the input. Under the
history containing $x^\star$, $\mathsf{P}$ finds it and writes nothing; under the other, $\mathsf{P}$
writes. If $\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{op}}$ reveals whether an operation occurred, the presence or absence
of the write is the distinguishing bit. If instead $\mathsf{P}$ always writes but $\Pi$'s leakage
reveals \emph{content}, choose the histories so that $\mathsf{P}$'s write lands in different
partitions under the two, and the region index distinguishes. Either way
$\AdvR_{\Pi,\mathsf{P},\mathcal{A}}$ is non-negligible. Mnemos-P satisfies \cref{thm:main} yet has
both leakages --- it flushes on demand, so a write's occurrence shows, and it places by content ---
so it is dual-epoch secure and not RDE.
\end{proof}

The proposition's hypothesis excludes exactly the stores whose disk behaviour is a function of the
clock alone. For those the composition goes through, and this is the positive result the submission
lacked.

\begin{theorem}[Composition: a clock-only store is reactively secure]
\label{thm:reactive}
Let $\Pi$ satisfy S1 and S2 and, in place of S3--S4, the clock conditions
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{C1 (clocked schedule).} The set of disk locations written at each flush is a function
of the flush index alone --- a deterministic sweep of $s$ slots per flush from a start fixed at
unlock --- and a flush occurs every $\Delta$ while unlocked whether or not anything was staged.
\item \textbf{C2 (fresh sealed writes).} Every written slot is a fresh authenticated ciphertext of
the fixed slot length, live or free alike.
\item \textbf{C3 (no post-unlock reads).} After the single sequential read at unlock, a
$\mathsf{search}$ issues no disk access; ranking and payload retrieval are served from the locked
arena.
\end{itemize}
Then for every feedback agent $\mathsf{P}$, $\Pi$ is reactively $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clock}}$-dual-epoch
secure, where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clock}}$ reveals only the unlock, lock and flush times.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Two hybrids as in \cref{thm:main} replace the PRF and then every ciphertext with a uniform string of
the slot length (C2, S1--S2). In the resulting game the snapshot is fixed-length uniform strings
(independent of $b$), and each flush writes the slot range named by C1's cursor --- a function of the
flush index, not of anything $\mathsf{P}$ did, wrote, or read. By C3 no $\mathsf{search}$ adds a disk
event, so $\mathsf{P}$'s reads are invisible and the branch $\mathsf{P}$ takes never reaches the
transcript. The whole view is therefore the unlock/lock/flush clock and uniform bytes, which a
simulator holding only $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{clock}}$ reproduces without $\mathsf{P}$, without the
inputs, and without $b$. The full argument, including the sealed journal that carries staged records
between flushes and is itself rewritten on the clock, is in the supplementary material.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Mnemos-M is reactively secure]
\label{cor:m-reactive}
The memory-resident store of \cref{sec:mnemos-m}, with its deterministic sweep flush, in-memory
search, and clocked schedule, satisfies C1--C3 and is reactively dual-epoch secure against every
feedback agent. Mnemos-P does not (\cref{prop:derived}), so RDE strictly refines dual-epoch security:
$\text{RDE} \Rightarrow \text{DE}$, and $\text{DE} \not\Rightarrow \text{RDE}$.
\end{corollary}

The separation is not only formal. \Cref{sec:eval-reactive} plays the reactive game against both
designs and a graded series of ablations, and finds the clock-only store at chance while every
store that flushes on a write, or places by content, is fully distinguished --- the empirical form
